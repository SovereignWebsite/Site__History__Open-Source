Connect the dots

Two histories, kept as two sets of facts. Here is where they meet: the same people, the same tools, the same ideas. Each line in the graph is a sourced fact on one side or the other.

Ward Cunningham and the wiki

One person stands in both histories. Ward Cunningham started the first wiki in 1995, signed the Agile Manifesto in 2001 (he is credited with its website), and later worked at the Eclipse Foundation on its open source committer community. The Snowbird meeting itself was planned on a wiki.

1995 Free software

Cunningham confirmed in correspondence with a dictionary editor that he named the technology WikiWikiWeb in 1995, after the Hawaiian word for quick.

I chose to call the technology WikiWikiWeb.

Primary source: own-post Correspondence on the Etymology of Wiki, Cunningham & Cunningham, Inc. (c2.com), by Ward Cunningham, published November 2003. Read 27 November 2026.

In the same reply, asked "In 1995?", he answers "Yes."

Connects: Ward Cunningham, WikiWikiWeb

25 March 1995 Free software

Ward Cunningham started the first wiki, the WikiWikiWeb, on 25 March 1995 as a supplement to the Portland Pattern Repository.

This is the first ever wiki site, founded as an automated supplement to the PortlandPatternRepository.

Primary source: archive WikiHistory (page of the original wiki, WikiWikiWeb), Cunningham & Cunningham, Inc. (c2.com), by Ward Cunningham and wiki contributors. Read 27 November 2026.

From the WikiHistory page of the original wiki, read from the site's page data (JSON). The page opens "This began on March 25, 1995." and continues in the first person ("I created the site"); it is a wiki page and so could have been edited by others.

Connects: Ward Cunningham, WikiWikiWeb

Release early, release often

Eric Raymond's rule for open source projects and the Manifesto's principle of frequent delivery say much the same thing, a few years apart. The sources read here do not show one borrowing from the other; they show two communities reaching the same habit.

May 1997 Free software

Eric Raymond's essay "The Cathedral and the Bazaar", first presented in May 1997, contrasted closed "cathedral" development with the open "bazaar" style of Linux.

I discuss these theories in terms of two fundamentally different development styles, the ``cathedral'' model of most of the commercial world versus the ``bazaar'' model of the Linux world.

Primary source: document The Cathedral and the Bazaar (title page, revision history and abstract), Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), by Eric S. Raymond, published May 1997. Read 27 November 2026.

The date is from the essay's revision history, which records the first official presentation at the Linux Kongress in May 1997. The text read is version 3.0 (2000). www.catb.org did not answer; catb.org over http did.

Connects: Eric S. Raymond, Linus Torvalds, The Cathedral and the Bazaar

Link to this fact

May 1997 Free software

Lesson 7 of "The Cathedral and the Bazaar" is to release early, release often and listen to your customers, which Raymond drew from how Torvalds ran Linux.

7. Release early. Release often. And listen to your customers.

Primary source: document The Cathedral and the Bazaar: Release Early, Release Often, Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), by Eric S. Raymond, published May 1997. Read 27 November 2026.

Text read is version 3.0 of the essay (2000); the lesson is commonly cited from the 1997 original.

Connects: Eric S. Raymond, Linus Torvalds, The Cathedral and the Bazaar

Link to this fact

May 1997 Free software

Raymond coined "Linus's Law": given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow.

Or, less formally, ``Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow.'' I dub this: ``Linus's Law''.

Primary source: document The Cathedral and the Bazaar: Release Early, Release Often, Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), by Eric S. Raymond, published May 1997. Read 27 November 2026.

The wording and the name are Raymond's, not Torvalds's. Text read is version 3.0 of the essay.

Connects: Eric S. Raymond, Linus Torvalds, The Cathedral and the Bazaar

Link to this fact

In the history of Agile

  • 1997: Raymond's essay made frequent release a rule of open source development, in words now often shortened to "release early, release often". Source: The Cathedral and the Bazaar: Release Early, Release Often
  • 21 May 1997: The revision history of Eric Raymond's The Cathedral and the Bazaar records its first official presentation on 21 May 1997 at the Linux Kongress. Source: The Cathedral and the Bazaar (version 3.0, title page and revision history)
  • 2001: One principle asks for working software to be delivered every couple of weeks to couple of months, the shorter the better. Source: Principles behind the Agile Manifesto

Fowler counts open source among the new methods

In July 2000, seven months before Snowbird, Martin Fowler's survey of the new lightweight methodologies had a section on open source and pointed readers to Raymond's essay. It is the clearest direct citation from one history to the other.

July 2000 The label

In the original July 2000 version of "The New Methodology", Martin Fowler listed open source alongside the new lightweight methods, saying much of its way of working applied to closed source projects too.

However there is a definite way of doing things in the open source community, and much of their approach is as applicable to closed source projects as it is to open source.

Primary source: own-post The New Methodology (Original), martinfowler.com, by Martin Fowler, published 21 July 2000. Read 27 November 2026.

Fowler republished the original text himself; the current version of the article no longer has the section.

Connects: Martin Fowler, The New Methodology

July 2000 The label

Fowler picked out the same point Raymond had made in "Linus's Law": in open source, debugging can be done by many people in parallel.

A particular feature of open source development is that debugging is highly parallelizable.

Primary source: own-post The New Methodology (Original), martinfowler.com, by Martin Fowler, published 21 July 2000. Read 27 November 2026.

Connects: Martin Fowler, Eric S. Raymond, The New Methodology, The Cathedral and the Bazaar

  • July 2000: Fowler's July 2000 survey of the new methodologies had a section on open source, saying much of that community's way of working applied to closed source projects too. Source: The New Methodology (Original)
  • July 2000: In the same section Fowler pointed readers to Eric Raymond's The Cathedral and the Bazaar as the most famous description of the open source process. Source: The New Methodology (Original)

A text made to be copied

The Manifesto carries its own copying notice: anyone may copy it in any form, but only whole. That is not an open source licence, since it allows no changes, but it shares the wish to be passed on that the GNU licences wrote into law for software.

March 1985 Free software

The GNU Manifesto of 1985 set out the rule that later became copyleft: anyone may modify and redistribute GNU, but nobody may restrict its further redistribution.

Everyone will be permitted to modify and redistribute GNU, but no distributor will be allowed to restrict its further redistribution.

Primary source: document The GNU Manifesto, GNU Project (Free Software Foundation), by Richard Stallman, published 1985. Read 27 November 2026.

The month, March 1985, is given by the GNU Project's overview page ("the GNU Manifesto was published in March 1985"); the manifesto page itself says only 1985.

Connects: Richard Stallman, GNU Project, The GNU Manifesto

Link to this fact

February 1989 Free software

Version 1 of the GNU General Public License, dated February 1989, states its purpose as guaranteeing the freedom to share and change free software.

our General Public License is intended to guarantee your freedom to share and change free software--to make sure the software is free for all its users.

Primary source: licence GNU General Public License, version 1, Free Software Foundation, published February 1989. Read 27 November 2026.

Connects: Free Software Foundation, GNU General Public License

February 2001 Hostility and adoption

The Manifesto for Agile Software Development carries a notice that it may be freely copied in any form, but only in its entirety.

© 2001, the above authors this declaration may be freely copied in any form, but only in its entirety through this notice.

Primary source: document Manifesto for Agile Software Development, agilemanifesto.org, by The seventeen authors, published February 2001. Read 27 November 2026.

This permits verbatim copying only, so it is not an open source or free culture licence; the parallel is in the wish to be copied.

Connects: Kent Beck, Ward Cunningham, Martin Fowler, Manifesto for Agile Software Development

JUnit and continuous integration

Extreme Programming's practices of testing and continuous integration spread as freely shared tools: JUnit, written by Kent Beck and Erich Gamma, then CruiseControl, and later the community-run Jenkins.

1997 Free software

Kent Beck and Erich Gamma wrote the first version of the JUnit testing framework together on a flight to the OOPSLA conference in 1997.

JUnit was born on a flight from Zurich to the 1997 OOPSLA in Atlanta. Kent was flying with Erich Gamma, and what else were two geeks to do on a long flight but program?

Secondary source: own-post Xunit, martinfowler.com, by Martin Fowler, published 17 January 2006. Read 27 November 2026.

Martin Fowler's account, written in 2006; he says he was one of the first users.

Connects: Kent Beck, Erich Gamma, Martin Fowler, JUnit

Link to this fact

Git, GitHub and the pull request

Git was made in 2005 for the Linux kernel. GitHub added the pull request in 2008, and Fowler now describes pull requests, which grew up in open source projects, as widely used on software projects of every kind.

2005 Hostility and adoption

Git was created in 2005 by Linus Torvalds and the Linux kernel community after they lost free use of the proprietary BitKeeper tool.

In 2005, the relationship between the community that developed the Linux kernel and the commercial company that developed BitKeeper broke down, and the tool’s free-of-charge status was revoked. This prompted the Linux development community (and in particular Linus Torvalds, the creator of Linux) to develop their own tool

Secondary source: book Pro Git, 1.2 Getting Started: A Short History of Git, git-scm.com (Apress), by Scott Chacon and Ben Straub. Read 27 November 2026.

From the Pro Git book hosted on the Git project's site. Torvalds's own 2005 messages on lore.kernel.org were behind a bot challenge and could not be read.

Connects: Linus Torvalds, Git

Link to this fact

Last night I pushed out a feature Tom and I have been talking about since day one: pull requests.

Primary source: own-post Oh yeah, there's pull requests now, The GitHub Blog, by Chris Wanstrath, published 23 February 2008. Read 27 November 2026.

DevOps

DevOps appears in both histories around 2009: on the Agile side as the carrying of frequent delivery into operations, on the open source side as a practice described at the time as Agile and Lean applied to operations work.

2009 Mainstream

The first devopsdays conference was held in Ghent, Belgium, in 2009.

The first devopsdays was held in Ghent, Belgium in 2009.

Primary source: document About devopsdays, devopsdays. Read 27 November 2026.

It is usually said that this conference gave the DevOps movement its name; that is not stated on this page and is not claimed here.

Connects: First devopsdays

Link to this fact

2 August 2010 Mainstream

An early practitioner definition of DevOps (2010) traces it to "agile infrastructure": applying Agile and Lean approaches to operations work.

DevOps emerged from the collision of two major related trends. The first was called “agile infrastructure” or “agile operations”; it sprang from applying Agile and Lean approaches to operations work.

Secondary source: reporting What Is DevOps?, the agile admin, by Ernest Mueller, published 2 August 2010. Read 27 November 2026.

A practitioner's blog, used as a contemporary secondary description; it is one view of the origins.

Link to this fact

Ruby and the Pragmatic Programmers

Two Manifesto authors, Dave Thomas and Andy Hunt, co-wrote Programming Ruby, and Thomas later put the word agile in the title of his book on Ruby on Rails. No fact on the open source side covers Ruby yet; this meeting point has one side only.

In the history of Agile

  • 2001: Andrew Hunt was a partner in The Pragmatic Programmers and co-author of The Pragmatic Programmer and of Programming Ruby. Source: Authors: The Agile Manifesto
  • 4 March 2014: Thomas noted that he had himself put the word in a book title, Agile Web Development with Rails, because of the agility Ruby on Rails gave him. Source: Agile is Dead (Long Live Agility)
