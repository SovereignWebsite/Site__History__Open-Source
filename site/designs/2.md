# The history of open source

From shared tapes to a contested label: how software that anyone may read, change and pass on came to run the world, told through the documents and the words of the people who were there.

Software was shared before it was sold: IBM shipped its early programs as source code and its users, organised from 1955 as SHARE, swapped their changes [1] [2]. Unix was born at Bell Labs in 1969 [3] and Berkeley began sending out its own distribution in 1977 [4]. The opposite view arrived in 1976, when Bill Gates told hobbyists that most of them were stealing their software [5].

In September 1983 Richard Stallman announced GNU, a complete Unix-compatible system to be given away [6], and gave an ethical reason for it [7]. The Free Software Foundation followed in 1985 [8], and the General Public License in 1989 used copyright to keep programs free [9] [10]. Linus Torvalds announced a hobby kernel in 1991 [11] and put it under the GNU copyleft [12]; Debian wrote down what "free" meant in 1997 [13].

The words "open source" date from February 1998 [14]. Netscape had just announced it would publish its browser's source [15]; Christine Peterson proposed the term [16], Eric Raymond campaigned for it [17], and he and Bruce Perens founded the Open Source Initiative with a definition adapted from Debian's guidelines [18] [19]. Stallman does not accept the new name, because in his view it leaves out freedom [20], and within a year Perens himself wrote that it was time to talk about free software again [21].

Microsoft treated the movement as a threat: a leaked 1998 memorandum called open source a direct, short-term revenue and platform threat [22]; in 2001 Jim Allchin called it an intellectual-property destroyer [23] and Steve Ballmer said Linux was a cancer in an intellectual property sense [24]; in 2007 the company claimed 235 of its patents were infringed [25]. Meanwhile others bought in: IBM pledged $1 billion to Linux [26], Sun freed StarOffice and Java [27] [28], Google released Android [29], and Git and GitHub changed how the work is done [30] [31].

Then Microsoft turned: it contributed code to the Linux kernel under the GPL in 2009 [32]; under Satya Nadella it showed a slide reading "Microsoft loves Linux" in 2014 [33], opened .NET [34], joined the Linux Foundation [35], bought GitHub for $7.5 billion [36] and pledged 60,000 patents to protect Linux [37]. In 2020 its president said it had been on the wrong side of history [38].

Since 2018 the argument has been over what the label still means: companies whose products were sold as cloud services by others have left open source licences [39] [40] [41] [42], the OSI has said their new licences are not open source [43], open alternatives have been set up under the Linux Foundation [44] [45] and two of the companies have come back [46] [47]. Heartbleed, Log4Shell and the xz backdoor showed how much rests on a few maintainers [48] [49] [50], the European Union has legislated [51], and the OSI's definition of open source AI is disputed [52] [53].

## Where things stand

The licence question is open again: MongoDB (2018), Elastic (2021), HashiCorp (2023) and Redis (2024) each moved a widely used product from an open source licence to one that restricts offering it as a service [39] [54] [40] [41] [42]. The Open Source Initiative holds that such licences are not open source, because the Open Source Definition forbids restricting fields of use [43] [55], and MongoDB's own FAQ says the SSPL is not OSI-approved [56].

Two of the changes were answered by a new project under the Linux Foundation: OpenTofu as an alternative to Terraform [44] and Valkey continuing Redis 7.2.4 [45]. Two companies have since added an OSI-approved licence back: Elastic in August 2024 [46] [57] and Redis in May 2025, writing that the SSPL "is not truly open source" [47] [58].

Security is now handled through shared bodies: after Heartbleed the Linux Foundation set up the Core Infrastructure Initiative [48] [59], which was folded into the Open Source Security Foundation in 2020 [60]. Log4Shell in 2021 was rated 10.0, critical [49], and in 2024 a backdoor was found in the upstream xz library [50].

Regulation has arrived: the European Union's Cyber Resilience Act entered into force on 10 December 2024 [51]; it covers open source only where it is supplied commercially [61] and creates the category of open source software steward [62], which the OSI welcomed [63].

The newest dispute is over artificial intelligence: the OSI published its Open Source AI Definition 1.0 on 28 October 2024 [52]; it asks for detailed information about training data rather than the data itself [64]. The Free Software Foundation says its own criteria will require the raw training data [65], and the Software Freedom Conservancy says the definition should not have been published yet [53].

By GitHub's own count, more than 180 million developers now use its platform [66], and in 2024 they made more than 5.2 billion contributions, a figure that includes private projects [67]. Microsoft, which owns GitHub [68], released the Windows Subsystem for Linux as open source in May 2025 [69].
