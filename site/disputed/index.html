What is disputed

A history that hides its arguments is not honest. Here is what is contested, who says what, and what the sources we read actually say.

"Free software" versus "open source"

The two terms cover almost the same programs but not the same idea1. Raymond argued in 1998 that "free software" was ambiguous and made business nervous2. Stallman holds that open source values practical advantage and leaves out freedom, and the GNU Project does not use the term13. Perens, who wrote the Open Source Definition, said in 1999 that the two were the same thing but that open source had played down the freedoms45. The dispute is one of values, not of fact1.

8 February 1998 The label

Raymond's stated reason for the new label was pragmatic: "free software" was ambiguous and made business people nervous.

Second, the term makes a lot of corporate types nervous. While this does not intrinsically bother me in the least, we now have a pragmatic interest in converting these people rather than thumbing our noses at them.

Primary source: own-post Goodbye, "free software"; hello, "open source", Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), by Eric S. Raymond, published 8 February 1998. Read 27 November 2026.

Who coined "open source", and when

The OSI credits Christine Peterson with suggesting the term6, and she has published her own account of coming up with it7. Raymond's call of 8 February 1998 says "we came up with" the label and does not name her8. The date of the meeting is not settled by the sources: the OSI says 3 February 19986, while Peterson's narrative says 5 February9 and Raymond's text also points to 5 February8. Peterson says it was Todd Anderson who put the term to the meeting9.

Between meetings that week, I was still focused on the need for a better name and came up with the term "open source software."

Primary source: own-post How I coined the term 'open source', Opensource.com (Red Hat), by Christine Peterson, published 1 February 2018. Read 27 November 2026.

Her account was written on 2 January 2006 and published on Opensource.com in February 2018.

The sentence as reported is longer: "Linux is a cancer that attaches itself in an intellectual property sense to everything it touches"11, and his explanation was about the terms of the licence12. The short form is a headline10. The words come from a Chicago Sun-Times interview of 1 June 2001, which could not be read for this site; they are quoted here from The Register's report of the next day11. In 2016 Ballmer was reported as saying the position had been right for its time13, wording that is the reporter's paraphrase.

The claim that free and open source software infringes 235 Microsoft patents is often put in Ballmer's mouth14. Fortune's article attributes the figure to Brad Smith and Horacio Gutierrez14; Ballmer is quoted in the same article on playing by the same rules15. Whether source-available licences are open source

MongoDB presented the SSPL as an open source licence and submitted it to the OSI1920. The OSI says it is not open source, because it restricts a field of use, and that it was withdrawn from review212223. The companies' own later statements concede the point about the label: MongoDB's FAQ says the SSPL is not OSI-approved24, Elastic said an OSI-approved licence was what let it use the term again25, and Redis wrote that the SSPL is not truly open source26. The opponents of HashiCorp's change call the Business Source License non-open source27; HashiCorp's own statement could not be read. What remains disputed is whether the OSI's definition should be the only test of the label21.

Stallman's role and the name "GNU/Linux"

The GNU Project says that by 1990 it had written or found every major part of a free system except the kernel, and that Linux completed it, so the combined system should be called GNU/Linux32. "Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow"

Raymond named this "Linus's Law", but the wording is his own35. Heartbleed, Log4Shell and the xz backdoor are the usual evidence that widely used code does not always have enough eyes on it363738; the xz backdoor was also found by one person who noticed odd behaviour and traced it38.

May 1997 Free software

Raymond coined "Linus's Law": given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow.

Or, less formally, ``Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow.'' I dub this: ``Linus's Law''.
