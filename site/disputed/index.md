# What is disputed

A history that hides its arguments is not honest. Here is what is contested, who says what, and what the sources we read actually say.

## "Free software" versus "open source"

The two terms cover almost the same programs but not the same idea [1]. Raymond argued in 1998 that "free software" was ambiguous and made business nervous [2]. Stallman holds that open source values practical advantage and leaves out freedom, and the GNU Project does not use the term [1] [3]. Perens, who wrote the Open Source Definition, said in 1999 that the two were the same thing but that open source had played down the freedoms [4] [5]. The dispute is one of values, not of fact [1].

### 8 February 1998: Raymond's stated reason for the new label was pragmatic: "free software" was ambiguous and made business people nervous.

> Second, the term makes a lot of corporate types nervous. While this does not intrinsically bother me in the least, we now have a pragmatic interest in converting these people rather than thumbing our noses at them.

- Source (primary, own-post): [Goodbye, "free software"; hello, "open source"](http://catb.org/~esr/open-source.html), Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), published 8 February 1998. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Eric S. Raymond
- Fact id: `raymond-free-software-term-has-to-go`

### 8 February 1998: On 8 February 1998 Eric Raymond publicly called on the community to replace the term "free software" with "open source", saying the group had come up with the label together.

> I brainstormed this with some Silicon Valley fans of Linux (including Larry Augustin of the Linux International board of directors) the day after my meeting with Netscape (Feb 5th). We kicked around and discarded several alternatives, and we came up with a replacement label we all liked: "open source".

- Source (primary, own-post): [Goodbye, "free software"; hello, "open source"](http://catb.org/~esr/open-source.html), Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), published 8 February 1998. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Raymond writes "we came up with"; he does not name Peterson in this passage. The date of 8 February is from his own preface to the document.
- Connects: Eric S. Raymond
- Fact id: `raymond-goodbye-free-software-1998`

### 2007: Stallman's position is that free software is a movement for users' freedom while open source values mainly practical advantage, and that the GNU Project therefore does not use the term "open source".

> The free software movement campaigns for freedom for the users of computing; it is a movement for freedom and justice. By contrast, the open source idea values mainly practical advantage and does not campaign for principles. This is why we do not agree with open source, and do not use that term.

- Source (primary, own-post): [Why Open Source Misses the Point of Free Software](http://www.gnu.org/philosophy/open-source-misses-the-point.html), GNU Project (Free Software Foundation), published 2007. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Dated to the first copyright year of the essay (2007); the essay has been revised since and the current text was read.
- Connects: Richard Stallman, Free Software Foundation, Why Open Source Misses the Point of Free Software
- Fact id: `stallman-open-source-misses-point`

### 1998: Richard Stallman describes 1998 as the year part of the free software community splintered off to campaign under the name "open source".

> In 1998, a part of the free software community splintered off and began campaigning in the name of “open source.”

- Source (primary, own-post): [Why Open Source Misses the Point of Free Software](http://www.gnu.org/philosophy/open-source-misses-the-point.html), GNU Project (Free Software Foundation), published 2007. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: From Stallman's essay, whose copyright notice begins in 2007.
- Connects: Richard Stallman, Why Open Source Misses the Point of Free Software
- Fact id: `stallman-1998-splinter`

### 17 February 1999: A year after co-founding the OSI, Bruce Perens wrote that open source had played down the freedoms of free software and that it was time to talk about free software again.

> Most hackers know that Free Software and Open Source are just two words for the same thing. Unfortunately, though, Open Source has de-emphasized the importance of the freedoms involved in Free Software.

- Source (primary, mailing-list): [It's Time to Talk about Free Software Again (email to the editor, as published)](https://static.lwn.net/1999/0218/a/bp.html), LWN.net, published 17 February 1999. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Read in the copy of his email of 17 February 1999 published by LWN.net; the Debian mailing-list archive presented a proof-of-work challenge and could not be read.
- Connects: Bruce Perens, Open Source Initiative, It's Time to Talk about Free Software Again
- Fact id: `perens-free-software-again-1999`

### 17 February 1999: In the same message Perens said he would go on promoting software that fits the Open Source Definition, but independently of the Open Source Initiative.

> I'll be working to promote software that fits that definition, but independently from the Open Source Initiative.

- Source (primary, mailing-list): [It's Time to Talk about Free Software Again (email to the editor, as published)](https://static.lwn.net/1999/0218/a/bp.html), LWN.net, published 17 February 1999. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Bruce Perens, Eric S. Raymond, Open Source Initiative, It's Time to Talk about Free Software Again
- Fact id: `perens-leaves-osi-1999`

### 1996: The GNU Project explains that "free" in free software is a matter of liberty and not of price.

> you should think of “free” as in “free speech,” not as in “free beer.”

- Source (primary, document): [What is Free Software? (The Free Software Definition)](http://www.gnu.org/philosophy/free-sw.html), GNU Project (Free Software Foundation). Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The date is the first copyright year on the page (1996); the phrase is quoted from the current text.

## Who coined "open source", and when

The OSI credits Christine Peterson with suggesting the term [6], and she has published her own account of coming up with it [7]. Raymond's call of 8 February 1998 says "we came up with" the label and does not name her [8]. The date of the meeting is not settled by the sources: the OSI says 3 February 1998 [6], while Peterson's narrative says 5 February [9] and Raymond's text also points to 5 February [8]. Peterson says it was Todd Anderson who put the term to the meeting [9].

### February 1998: Christine Peterson, then executive director of the Foresight Institute, says she came up with the term "open source software" in the first week of February 1998.

> Between meetings that week, I was still focused on the need for a better name and came up with the term "open source software."

- Source (primary, own-post): [How I coined the term 'open source'](https://opensource.com/article/18/2/coining-term-open-source-software), Opensource.com (Red Hat), published 1 February 2018. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Her account was written on 2 January 2006 and published on Opensource.com in February 2018.
- Connects: Christine Peterson, Foresight Institute
- Fact id: `peterson-coins-open-source-1998`

### 5 February 1998: In Peterson's account the term was adopted at a strategy meeting at VA Research on 5 February 1998, where Todd Anderson put it forward and Eric Raymond endorsed it.

> Later that week, on February 5, 1998, a group was assembled at VA Research to brainstorm on strategy. [...] Todd advocated the "open source" model, and Eric endorsed this.

- Source (primary, own-post): [How I coined the term 'open source'](https://opensource.com/article/18/2/coining-term-open-source-software), Opensource.com (Red Hat), published 1 February 2018. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Disputed. The date is contested between the sources. Peterson's narrative says 5 February 1998, and Raymond's call of 8 February 1998 also points to 5 February; the Open Source Initiative's history says 3 February 1998, and the editor's introduction to Peterson's own article calls 3 February the anniversary.
- Connects: Christine Peterson, Eric S. Raymond, Todd Anderson, The strategy session that adopted "open source"
- Fact id: `open-source-meeting-peterson-account`

### 3 February 1998: The Open Source Initiative's own history says the "open source" label was created at a strategy session in Palo Alto on 3 February 1998, and credits Christine Peterson with suggesting it.

> The “open source” label was created at a strategy session held on February 3rd, 1998 in Palo Alto, California, shortly after the announcement of the release of the Netscape source code.

- Source (primary, document): [History of the OSI](https://opensource.org/history), Open Source Initiative. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Disputed. The OSI page gives 3 February 1998; Peterson's first-hand narrative gives 5 February 1998 (see open-source-meeting-peterson-account). The same OSI page says the term was "originally suggested by Christine Peterson".
- Connects: Christine Peterson, Open Source Initiative, The strategy session that adopted "open source"
- Fact id: `open-source-label-osi-account`

### 8 February 1998: On 8 February 1998 Eric Raymond publicly called on the community to replace the term "free software" with "open source", saying the group had come up with the label together.

> I brainstormed this with some Silicon Valley fans of Linux (including Larry Augustin of the Linux International board of directors) the day after my meeting with Netscape (Feb 5th). We kicked around and discarded several alternatives, and we came up with a replacement label we all liked: "open source".

- Source (primary, own-post): [Goodbye, "free software"; hello, "open source"](http://catb.org/~esr/open-source.html), Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), published 8 February 1998. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Raymond writes "we came up with"; he does not name Peterson in this passage. The date of 8 February is from his own preface to the document.
- Connects: Eric S. Raymond
- Fact id: `raymond-goodbye-free-software-1998`


## What Steve Ballmer said about Linux in 2001

The usual quotation is "Linux is a cancer" [10]. The sentence as reported is longer: "Linux is a cancer that attaches itself in an intellectual property sense to everything it touches" [11], and his explanation was about the terms of the licence [12]. The short form is a headline [10]. The words come from a Chicago Sun-Times interview of 1 June 2001, which could not be read for this site; they are quoted here from The Register's report of the next day [11]. In 2016 Ballmer was reported as saying the position had been right for its time [13], wording that is the reporter's paraphrase.

### 1 June 2001: Steve Ballmer, Microsoft's chief executive, told the Chicago Sun-Times on 1 June 2001 that Linux is a cancer that attaches itself in an intellectual property sense to everything it touches.

> "Linux is a cancer that attaches itself in an intellectual property sense to everything it touches,"

- Source (secondary, reporting): [Ballmer: 'Linux is a cancer'](https://www.theregister.com/2001/06/02/ballmer_linux_is_a_cancer/), The Register, published 2 June 2001. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The words were said in an interview with the Chicago Sun-Times published on 1 June 2001. That article could not be read: the newspaper's old address returns 404 and the Internet Archive was unreachable from this machine. The quote is taken from The Register's report of 2 June 2001, which quotes the interview. No transcript is known, so the newspaper is the record of the words and The Register is a second-hand record.
- Connects: Steve Ballmer, Microsoft, Steve Ballmer's Chicago Sun-Times interview
- Fact id: `ballmer-cancer-2001`

### 1 June 2001: Ballmer's explanation of the remark was about licensing: he said that using any open source software obliges a company to make the rest of its software open source.

> "The way the license is written, if you use any open-source software, you have to make the rest of your software open source,"

- Source (secondary, reporting): [Ballmer: 'Linux is a cancer'](https://www.theregister.com/2001/06/02/ballmer_linux_is_a_cancer/), The Register, published 2 June 2001. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: As quoted by The Register from the Sun-Times interview. The Register's reporter argues in the same article that this misreads the GPL.
- Connects: Steve Ballmer, Microsoft, GNU General Public License, Steve Ballmer's Chicago Sun-Times interview
- Fact id: `ballmer-cancer-licence-context-2001`

### 2 June 2001: The remark is usually repeated in the short form "Linux is a cancer", which is the headline The Register gave its report, and which drops the qualifier about intellectual property.

> Ballmer: 'Linux is a cancer'

- Source (secondary, reporting): [Ballmer: 'Linux is a cancer'](https://www.theregister.com/2001/06/02/ballmer_linux_is_a_cancer/), The Register, published 2 June 2001. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Usually paraphrased. The short form is a headline, not the sentence Ballmer is reported to have said. The fuller sentence, as The Register quotes it, is "Linux is a cancer that attaches itself in an intellectual property sense to everything it touches".
- Connects: Steve Ballmer, Steve Ballmer's Chicago Sun-Times interview
- Fact id: `ballmer-cancer-short-form`

### 9 March 2016: In March 2016 Steve Ballmer said his earlier stance on Linux had been right for its time but that the threat from Linux was now "in the rearview mirror".

> Ballmer said the position was right for the time, but the threat from Linux was now "in the rearview mirror."

- Source (secondary, reporting): [Former Microsoft CEO Ballmer does about-face on Linux technology](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2016/03/11/former-microsoft-ceo-ballmer-does-about-face-on-linux-technology), Reuters (as carried by The Star, Malaysia), published 11 March 2016. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Usually paraphrased. A Reuters report, read as carried by The Star (Malaysia) because reuters.com returned 401; only the first two paragraphs were readable there. Only "in the rearview mirror" is a direct quotation; "right for the time" is the reporter's paraphrase. The remarks were made at a dinner hosted by Fortune on 9 March 2016; no transcript is known.
- Connects: Steve Ballmer, Microsoft
- Fact id: `ballmer-rearview-2016`


## Who made the 235 patents claim

The claim that free and open source software infringes 235 Microsoft patents is often put in Ballmer's mouth [14]. Fortune's article attributes the figure to Brad Smith and Horacio Gutierrez [14]; Ballmer is quoted in the same article on playing by the same rules [15]. Microsoft later acknowledged friction over patents when it joined the Open Invention Network [16].

### May 2007: In May 2007 Microsoft's general counsel Brad Smith and licensing chief Horacio Gutierrez told Fortune that free and open source software infringed 235 Microsoft patents.

> Revealing the precise figure for the first time, they state that FOSS infringes on no fewer than 235 Microsoft patents.

- Source (secondary, reporting): [Microsoft takes on the free world](https://fortune.com/article/microsoft-linux-intellectual-property/), Fortune, published 28 May 2007. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Disputed. The figure is often attributed to Steve Ballmer. In the Fortune article it is stated by Brad Smith and Horacio Gutierrez; Ballmer is quoted in the same article saying open source users must "play by the same rules as the rest of the business". Microsoft did not identify the patents, and the claim was never tested in court as far as the sources read here show.
- Connects: Brad Smith, Steve Ballmer, Microsoft
- Fact id: `microsoft-235-patents-2007`

### May 2007: In the same Fortune article Steve Ballmer said that users of free and open source software would have to play by the same rules as the rest of the business.

> FOSS patrons are going to have to “play by the same rules as the rest of the business,” he insists. “What’s fair is fair.”

- Source (secondary, reporting): [Microsoft takes on the free world](https://fortune.com/article/microsoft-linux-intellectual-property/), Fortune, published 28 May 2007. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Fortune is the record of the words, from its own interview; no transcript is published.
- Connects: Steve Ballmer, Microsoft
- Fact id: `ballmer-same-rules-2007`

### 10 October 2018: In the same post Microsoft acknowledged past friction with the open source community over patents.

> it is no secret that there has been friction in the past between Microsoft and the open source community over the issue of patents.

- Source (primary, own-post): [Microsoft joins Open Invention Network to help protect Linux and open source](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/microsoft-joins-open-invention-network-to-help-protect-linux-and-open-source/), Microsoft Azure Blog, published 10 October 2018. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Microsoft, Open Invention Network
- Fact id: `microsoft-oin-friction-2018`

## Whether source-available licences are open source

MongoDB presented the SSPL as an open source licence and submitted it to the OSI [19] [20]. The OSI says it is not open source, because it restricts a field of use, and that it was withdrawn from review [21] [22] [23]. The companies' own later statements concede the point about the label: MongoDB's FAQ says the SSPL is not OSI-approved [24], Elastic said an OSI-approved licence was what let it use the term again [25], and Redis wrote that the SSPL is not truly open source [26]. The opponents of HashiCorp's change call the Business Source License non-open source [27]; HashiCorp's own statement could not be read. What remains disputed is whether the OSI's definition should be the only test of the label [21].

### 16 October 2018: On 16 October 2018 MongoDB moved its Community Server from the AGPL to a new licence of its own, the Server Side Public License.

> issued a new software license, called Server Side Public License (SSPL), for MongoDB Community Server.

- Source (primary, press-release): [MongoDB Issues New Server Side Public License for MongoDB Community Server](https://www.mongodb.com/company/newsroom/press-releases/mongodb-issues-new-server-side-public-license-for-mongodb-community-server), MongoDB, Inc., published 16 October 2018. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The same release says MongoDB "submitted it to the Open Source Initiative for approval".
- Connects: MongoDB, Inc., Open Source Initiative, Server Side Public License
- Fact id: `mongodb-sspl-2018`

### 16 October 2018: MongoDB said the one substantive change in the SSPL was that anyone offering MongoDB as a service must open source the software used to run that service.

> The only substantive change is an explicit condition that any organization attempting to exploit MongoDB as a service must open source the software that it uses to offer such service.

- Source (primary, press-release): [MongoDB Issues New Server Side Public License for MongoDB Community Server](https://www.mongodb.com/company/newsroom/press-releases/mongodb-issues-new-server-side-public-license-for-mongodb-community-server), MongoDB, Inc., published 16 October 2018. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: MongoDB, Inc., Server Side Public License
- Fact id: `mongodb-sspl-cloud-reason-2018`

### 2018: MongoDB's own FAQ states that the SSPL has not been approved by the Open Source Initiative.

> The SSPL has not been approved by the OSI.

- Source (primary, document): [Server Side Public License FAQ](https://www.mongodb.com/legal/licensing/server-side-public-license/faq), MongoDB, Inc.. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The FAQ is undated; it is dated here to the year the licence was issued.
- Connects: MongoDB, Inc., Open Source Initiative, Server Side Public License
- Fact id: `sspl-not-osi-approved`

### 19 January 2021: On 19 January 2021 the Open Source Initiative stated that the SSPL is not an open source licence, calling such licences "fauxpen" source.

> Fauxpen source licenses allow a user to view the source code but do not allow other highly important rights protected by the Open Source Definition, such as the right to make use of the program for any field of endeavor.

- Source (primary, press-release): [The SSPL is Not an Open Source License](https://opensource.org/blog/the-sspl-is-not-an-open-source-license), Open Source Initiative, published 19 January 2021. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Open Source Initiative, Elastic, MongoDB, Inc., Server Side Public License, The Open Source Definition
- Fact id: `osi-sspl-not-open-source-2021`

### 19 January 2021: The OSI says the SSPL was submitted for its approval and then withdrawn when it became clear it would not be approved.

> This license was submitted to the Open Source Initiative for approval but later withdrawn by the license steward when it became clear that the license would not be approved.

- Source (primary, press-release): [The SSPL is Not an Open Source License](https://opensource.org/blog/the-sspl-is-not-an-open-source-license), Open Source Initiative, published 19 January 2021. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Open Source Initiative, MongoDB, Inc., Server Side Public License
- Fact id: `osi-sspl-withdrawn`

## The Open Source AI Definition

The OSI's definition requires detailed information about training data, not the data [28] [29]. The Free Software Foundation says a free machine learning application must provide the raw training data [30]. The Software Freedom Conservancy says the definition fails to require reproducibility and should have been held back [31].

### 28 October 2024: On 28 October 2024 the Open Source Initiative released version 1.0 of its Open Source AI Definition.

> After a year-long, global, community design process, the Open Source Definition (OSAID) v.1.0 is available for public use.

- Source (primary, press-release): [The Open Source Initiative Announces the Release of the Industry's First Open Source AI Definition](https://opensource.org/blog/the-open-source-initiative-announces-the-release-of-the-industrys-first-open-source-ai-definition), Open Source Initiative, published 28 October 2024. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The release sentence says "the Open Source Definition (OSAID)"; the document released is the Open Source AI Definition.
- Connects: Open Source Initiative, The Open Source AI Definition 1.0
- Fact id: `osaid-1-0-2024`

### 28 October 2024: The Open Source AI Definition requires detailed information about the training data, enough for a skilled person to build a substantially equivalent system, but not the training data itself.

> Data Information: Sufficiently detailed information about the data used to train the system so that a skilled person can build a substantially equivalent system.

- Source (primary, document): [The Open Source AI Definition 1.0](https://opensource.org/ai/open-source-ai-definition), Open Source Initiative, published 28 October 2024. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: That the data itself is not required is the reading of the definition's text and is the point its critics attack; see the Software Freedom Conservancy and FSF facts.
- Connects: Open Source Initiative, The Open Source AI Definition 1.0
- Fact id: `osaid-data-information`

### 22 October 2024: A week earlier the Free Software Foundation had said its own criteria for free machine learning applications would require the raw training data to carry the four freedoms.

> it is working on a statement of criteria for free machine learning applications, which will require the software, as well as the raw training data and associated scripts, to grant users the four freedoms.

- Source (primary, press-release): [FSF is working on freedom in machine learning applications](https://www.fsf.org/news/fsf-is-working-on-freedom-in-machine-learning-applications), Free Software Foundation, published 22 October 2024. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Free Software Foundation, The Open Source AI Definition 1.0
- Fact id: `fsf-ml-training-data-2024`

### 31 October 2024: The Software Freedom Conservancy's Bradley Kuhn criticised the definition for not requiring public disclosure of training sets, and said the OSI should not have published it yet.

> the OSAID fails to require reproducibility by the public of the scientific process of building these systems, because the OSAID fails to place sufficient requirements on the licensing and public disclosure of training sets

- Source (primary, own-post): [Open Source AI Definition Erodes the Meaning of "Open Source"](https://sfconservancy.org/blog/2024/oct/31/open-source-ai-definition-osaid-erodes-foss/), Software Freedom Conservancy, published 31 October 2024. Read 27 November 2026.

## Stallman's role and the name "GNU/Linux"

The GNU Project says that by 1990 it had written or found every major part of a free system except the kernel, and that Linux completed it, so the combined system should be called GNU/Linux [32]. Torvalds's own first announcement set his kernel beside GNU, "won't be big and professional like gnu" [33], and he adopted the GNU copyleft [34]. Only the GNU Project's side of the naming argument is sourced here [32]: no statement by Torvalds on the name was fetched.

### 1992: The GNU Project says that by 1990 it had every major component of its system except the kernel, and that Linux filled that gap from 1992.

> By 1990 we had either found or written all the major components except one—the kernel. Then Linux, a Unix-like kernel, was developed by Linus Torvalds in 1991 and made free software in 1992.

- Source (primary, document): [Overview of the GNU System](http://www.gnu.org/gnu/gnu-history.html), GNU Project (Free Software Foundation). Read 27 November 2026.

## "Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow"

Raymond named this "Linus's Law", but the wording is his own [35]. Heartbleed, Log4Shell and the xz backdoor are the usual evidence that widely used code does not always have enough eyes on it [36] [37] [38]; the xz backdoor was also found by one person who noticed odd behaviour and traced it [38].

### May 1997: Raymond coined "Linus's Law": given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow.

> Or, less formally, ``Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow.'' I dub this: ``Linus's Law''.

- Source (primary, document): [The Cathedral and the Bazaar: Release Early, Release Often](http://catb.org/~esr/writings/cathedral-bazaar/cathedral-bazaar/ar01s04.html), Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), published May 1997. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The wording and the name are Raymond's, not Torvalds's. Text read is version 3.0 of the essay.
- Connects: Eric S. Raymond, Linus Torvalds, The Cathedral and the Bazaar
- Fact id: `linuss-law-1997`

## What the April 1998 summit was called

The same meeting is the "Freeware Summit" in its advance announcement and the "Open Source Summit" a week later, by Peterson's account [39]; O'Reilly's press release speaks of open source pioneers [40]; the OSI's history calls it the "Free Software Summit" [6].

### April 1998: The April 1998 meeting was announced as the "Freeware Summit" and within a week was being called the "Open Source Summit".

> Announced in advance as the first "Freeware Summit," by April 14 it was referred to as the first "Open Source Summit."

- Source (primary, own-post): [How I coined the term 'open source'](https://opensource.com/article/18/2/coining-term-open-source-software), Opensource.com (Red Hat), published 1 February 2018. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The OSI history page calls the same meeting the "April 1998 Free Software Summit", so three names are in use for it.
- Connects: Tim O'Reilly, Christine Peterson, Freeware Summit / Open Source Summit
- Fact id: `freeware-summit-renamed-1998`
