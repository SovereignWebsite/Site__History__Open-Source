The label

1998 to 2000.

Netscape's decision to publish its browser source1 prompted a new name, "open source", proposed by Christine Peterson2 and promoted by Eric Raymond, Bruce Perens and Tim O'Reilly34. Stallman's movement kept the name free software5. Microsoft's internal view leaked in the Halloween Documents6, while Red Hat went public and IBM and Sun committed to open source789.

1998 The label

Criterion 6 of the Open Source Definition forbids a licence from restricting the fields in which a program may be used, including use in a business.

The license must not restrict anyone from making use of the program in a specific field of endeavor. For example, it may not restrict the program from being used in a business, or from being used for genetic research.

Primary source: document The Open Source Definition, Open Source Initiative. Read 27 November 2026.

Quoted from the current text of the definition. This is the clause the OSI cites against licences that restrict cloud providers.

Connects: Open Source Initiative, The Open Source Definition

22 January 1998 The label

On 22 January 1998 Netscape announced that it would publish the source code of the next generation of its Communicator browser.

MOUNTAIN VIEW, Calif. (January 22, 1998) — Netscape Communications Corporation (NASDAQ: NSCP) today announced bold plans to make the source code for the next generation of its highly popular Netscape Communicator client software available for free licensing on the Internet.

The same opening paragraph was forwarded to the Linux kernel mailing list on 22 January 1998. The Netscape original survives only in the Internet Archive, which could not be reached from this machine.

February 1998 The label

Christine Peterson, then executive director of the Foresight Institute, says she came up with the term "open source software" in the first week of February 1998.

Between meetings that week, I was still focused on the need for a better name and came up with the term "open source software."

Her account was written on 2 January 2006 and published on Opensource.com in February 2018.

3 February 1998 The label

The Open Source Initiative's own history says the "open source" label was created at a strategy session in Palo Alto on 3 February 1998, and credits Christine Peterson with suggesting it.

The “open source” label was created at a strategy session held on February 3rd, 1998 in Palo Alto, California, shortly after the announcement of the release of the Netscape source code.

Primary source: document History of the OSI, Open Source Initiative. Read 27 November 2026.

Disputed. The OSI page gives 3 February 1998; Peterson's first-hand narrative gives 5 February 1998 (see open-source-meeting-peterson-account). The same OSI page says the term was "originally suggested by Christine Peterson".

Connects: Christine Peterson, Open Source Initiative, The strategy session that adopted "open source"

5 February 1998 The label

In Peterson's account the term was adopted at a strategy meeting at VA Research on 5 February 1998, where Todd Anderson put it forward and Eric Raymond endorsed it.

Later that week, on February 5, 1998, a group was assembled at VA Research to brainstorm on strategy. [...] Todd advocated the "open source" model, and Eric endorsed this.

Primary source: own-post How I coined the term 'open source', Opensource.com (Red Hat), by Christine Peterson, published 1 February 2018. Read 27 November 2026.

Disputed. The date is contested between the sources. Peterson's narrative says 5 February 1998, and Raymond's call of 8 February 1998 also points to 5 February; the Open Source Initiative's history says 3 February 1998, and the editor's introduction to Peterson's own article calls 3 February the anniversary.

Connects: Christine Peterson, Eric S. Raymond, Todd Anderson, The strategy session that adopted "open source"

8 February 1998 The label

Raymond's stated reason for the new label was pragmatic: "free software" was ambiguous and made business people nervous.

Second, the term makes a lot of corporate types nervous. While this does not intrinsically bother me in the least, we now have a pragmatic interest in converting these people rather than thumbing our noses at them.

Primary source: own-post Goodbye, "free software"; hello, "open source", Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), by Eric S. Raymond, published 8 February 1998. Read 27 November 2026.

Connects: Eric S. Raymond

Link to this fact

8 February 1998 The label

On 8 February 1998 Eric Raymond publicly called on the community to replace the term "free software" with "open source", saying the group had come up with the label together.

I brainstormed this with some Silicon Valley fans of Linux (including Larry Augustin of the Linux International board of directors) the day after my meeting with Netscape (Feb 5th). We kicked around and discarded several alternatives, and we came up with a replacement label we all liked: "open source".

Primary source: own-post Goodbye, "free software"; hello, "open source", Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), by Eric S. Raymond, published 8 February 1998. Read 27 November 2026.

Raymond writes "we came up with"; he does not name Peterson in this passage. The date of 8 February is from his own preface to the document.

Connects: Eric S. Raymond

7 April 1998 The label

At a summit organised by Tim O'Reilly in Palo Alto on 7 April 1998, leading developers agreed that "open source" or "sourceware" best described their way of working.

An internal Microsoft memorandum of August 1998 judged open source software to be a direct, short-term revenue and platform threat to the company.

OSS poses a direct, short-term revenue and platform threat to Microsoft, particularly in server space.

17 February 1999 The label

A year after co-founding the OSI, Bruce Perens wrote that open source had played down the freedoms of free software and that it was time to talk about free software again.

Most hackers know that Free Software and Open Source are just two words for the same thing. Unfortunately, though, Open Source has de-emphasized the importance of the freedoms involved in Free Software.

Primary source: mailing-list It's Time to Talk about Free Software Again (email to the editor, as published), LWN.net, by Bruce Perens, published 17 February 1999. Read 27 November 2026.

Read in the copy of his email of 17 February 1999 published by LWN.net; the Debian mailing-list archive presented a proof-of-work challenge and could not be read.

Connects: Bruce Perens, Open Source Initiative, It's Time to Talk about Free Software Again

Fowler picked out the same point Raymond had made in "Linus's Law": in open source, debugging can be done by many people in parallel.

A particular feature of open source development is that debugging is highly parallelizable.

Primary source: own-post The New Methodology (Original), martinfowler.com, by Martin Fowler, published 21 July 2000. Read 27 November 2026.

Connects: Martin Fowler, Eric S. Raymond, The New Methodology, The Cathedral and the Bazaar

July 2000 The label

In the original July 2000 version of "The New Methodology", Martin Fowler listed open source alongside the new lightweight methods, saying much of its way of working applied to closed source projects too.

However there is a definite way of doing things in the open source community, and much of their approach is as applicable to closed source projects as it is to open source.

Primary source: own-post The New Methodology (Original), martinfowler.com, by Martin Fowler, published 21 July 2000. Read 27 November 2026.
