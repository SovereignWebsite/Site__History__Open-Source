The history of open source

From shared tapes to a contested label: how software that anyone may read, change and pass on came to run the world, told through the documents and the words of the people who were there.

Software was shared before it was sold: IBM shipped its early programs as source code and its users, organised from 1955 as SHARE, swapped their changes12. Unix was born at Bell Labs in 19693 and Berkeley began sending out its own distribution in 19774. The opposite view arrived in 1976, when Bill Gates told hobbyists that most of them were stealing their software5.

In September 1983 Richard Stallman announced GNU, a complete Unix-compatible system to be given away6, and gave an ethical reason for it7. The Free Software Foundation followed in 19858, and the General Public License in 1989 used copyright to keep programs free910. Linus Torvalds announced a hobby kernel in 199111 and put it under the GNU copyleft12; Debian wrote down what "free" meant in 199713.

The words "open source" date from February 199814. Netscape had just announced it would publish its browser's source15; Christine Peterson proposed the term16, Eric Raymond campaigned for it17, and he and Bruce Perens founded the Open Source Initiative with a definition adapted from Debian's guidelines1819. Stallman does not accept the new name, because in his view it leaves out freedom20, and within a year Perens himself wrote that it was time to talk about free software again21.

Microsoft treated the movement as a threat: a leaked 1998 memorandum called open source a direct, short-term revenue and platform threat22; in 2001 Jim Allchin called it an intellectual-property destroyer23 and Steve Ballmer said Linux was a cancer in an intellectual property sense24; in 2007 the company claimed 235 of its patents were infringed25. Meanwhile others bought in: IBM pledged $1 billion to Linux26, Sun freed StarOffice and Java2728, Google released Android29, and Git and GitHub changed how the work is done3031.

Then Microsoft turned: it contributed code to the Linux kernel under the GPL in 200932; under Satya Nadella it showed a slide reading "Microsoft loves Linux" in 201433, opened .NET34, joined the Linux Foundation35, bought GitHub for $7.5 billion36 and pledged 60,000 patents to protect Linux37. In 2020 its president said it had been on the wrong side of history38.

Since 2018 the argument has been over what the label still means: companies whose products were sold as cloud services by others have left open source licences39404142, the OSI has said their new licences are not open source43, open alternatives have been set up under the Linux Foundation4445 and two of the companies have come back4647. Heartbleed, Log4Shell and the xz backdoor showed how much rests on a few maintainers484950, the European Union has legislated51, and the OSI's definition of open source AI is disputed5253.

Where things stand

The licence question is open again: MongoDB (2018), Elastic (2021), HashiCorp (2023) and Redis (2024) each moved a widely used product from an open source licence to one that restricts offering it as a service3954404142. The Open Source Initiative holds that such licences are not open source, because the Open Source Definition forbids restricting fields of use4355, and MongoDB's own FAQ says the SSPL is not OSI-approved56.

Two of the changes were answered by a new project under the Linux Foundation: OpenTofu as an alternative to Terraform44 and Valkey continuing Redis 7.2.445. Two companies have since added an OSI-approved licence back: Elastic in August 20244657 and Redis in May 2025, writing that the SSPL "is not truly open source"4758.

Security is now handled through shared bodies: after Heartbleed the Linux Foundation set up the Core Infrastructure Initiative4859, which was folded into the Open Source Security Foundation in 202060. Log4Shell in 2021 was rated 10.0, critical49, and in 2024 a backdoor was found in the upstream xz library50.

Regulation has arrived: the European Union's Cyber Resilience Act entered into force on 10 December 202451; it covers open source only where it is supplied commercially61 and creates the category of open source software steward62, which the OSI welcomed63.

The newest dispute is over artificial intelligence: the OSI published its Open Source AI Definition 1.0 on 28 October 202452; it asks for detailed information about training data rather than the data itself64. The Free Software Foundation says its own criteria will require the raw training data65, and the Software Freedom Conservancy says the definition should not have been published yet53.

By GitHub's own count, more than 180 million developers now use its platform66, and in 2024 they made more than 5.2 billion contributions, a figure that includes private projects67. Microsoft, which owns GitHub68, released the Windows Subsystem for Linux as open source in May 202569.
