May 1997 Free software

Eric Raymond's essay "The Cathedral and the Bazaar", first presented in May 1997, contrasted closed "cathedral" development with the open "bazaar" style of Linux.

I discuss these theories in terms of two fundamentally different development styles, the ``cathedral'' model of most of the commercial world versus the ``bazaar'' model of the Linux world.

Primary source: document The Cathedral and the Bazaar (title page, revision history and abstract), Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), by Eric S. Raymond, published May 1997. Read 27 November 2026.

The date is from the essay's revision history, which records the first official presentation at the Linux Kongress in May 1997. The text read is version 3.0 (2000). www.catb.org did not answer; catb.org over http did.

Connects: Eric S. Raymond, Linus Torvalds, The Cathedral and the Bazaar

May 1997 Free software

Raymond coined "Linus's Law": given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow.

Or, less formally, ``Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow.'' I dub this: ``Linus's Law''.

Primary source: document The Cathedral and the Bazaar: Release Early, Release Often, Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), by Eric S. Raymond, published May 1997. Read 27 November 2026.

The wording and the name are Raymond's, not Torvalds's. Text read is version 3.0 of the essay.

Connects: Eric S. Raymond, Linus Torvalds, The Cathedral and the Bazaar

Link to this fact

May 1997 Free software

Lesson 7 of "The Cathedral and the Bazaar" is to release early, release often and listen to your customers, which Raymond drew from how Torvalds ran Linux.

7. Release early. Release often. And listen to your customers.

Primary source: document The Cathedral and the Bazaar: Release Early, Release Often, Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), by Eric S. Raymond, published May 1997. Read 27 November 2026.

Text read is version 3.0 of the essay (2000); the lesson is commonly cited from the 1997 original.

Connects: Eric S. Raymond, Linus Torvalds, The Cathedral and the Bazaar

January 1998 The label

Raymond reports that Netscape's chief technology officer, Eric Hahn, wrote to him that his essay had been a fundamental inspiration for the decision to release the source.

Your thinking and writings were fundamental inspirations to our decision.

Primary source: own-post The Cathedral and the Bazaar: Epilog: Netscape Embraces the Bazaar, Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), by Eric S. Raymond, published February 1998. Read 27 November 2026.

Usually paraphrased. The words are from a private email as quoted by Raymond in the epilogue of his own essay; no independent copy of the email is public, so this is Raymond's record of what Hahn wrote.

Connects: Eric S. Raymond, Netscape Communications, The Cathedral and the Bazaar

February 1998 The label

Eric Raymond and Bruce Perens founded the Open Source Initiative in late February 1998.

OSI was jointly founded by Eric Raymond and Bruce Perens in late February 1998, with Raymond as its first president, Perens as vice-president

Primary source: document History of the OSI, Open Source Initiative. Read 27 November 2026.

Connects: Eric S. Raymond, Bruce Perens, Open Source Initiative

February 1998 The label

Bruce Perens says in his own account that he edited the Debian guidelines into the Open Source Definition at Raymond's suggestion.

Raymond felt that the Debian Free Software Guidelines were the right document to define Open Source, but that they needed a more general name and the removal of Debian-specific references. I edited the Guidelines to form the Open Source Definition.

Primary source: own-post The Open Source Definition (in Open Sources: Voices from the Open Source Revolution), O'Reilly, by Bruce Perens, published January 1999. Read 27 November 2026.

Connects: Bruce Perens, Eric S. Raymond, The Open Source Definition, Debian Free Software Guidelines

5 February 1998 The label

In Peterson's account the term was adopted at a strategy meeting at VA Research on 5 February 1998, where Todd Anderson put it forward and Eric Raymond endorsed it.

Later that week, on February 5, 1998, a group was assembled at VA Research to brainstorm on strategy. [...] Todd advocated the "open source" model, and Eric endorsed this.

Primary source: own-post How I coined the term 'open source', Opensource.com (Red Hat), by Christine Peterson, published 1 February 2018. Read 27 November 2026.

Disputed. The date is contested between the sources. Peterson's narrative says 5 February 1998, and Raymond's call of 8 February 1998 also points to 5 February; the Open Source Initiative's history says 3 February 1998, and the editor's introduction to Peterson's own article calls 3 February the anniversary.

Connects: Christine Peterson, Eric S. Raymond, Todd Anderson, The strategy session that adopted "open source"

8 February 1998 The label

Raymond's stated reason for the new label was pragmatic: "free software" was ambiguous and made business people nervous.

Second, the term makes a lot of corporate types nervous. While this does not intrinsically bother me in the least, we now have a pragmatic interest in converting these people rather than thumbing our noses at them.

Primary source: own-post Goodbye, "free software"; hello, "open source", Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), by Eric S. Raymond, published 8 February 1998. Read 27 November 2026.

Connects: Eric S. Raymond

Link to this fact

8 February 1998 The label

On 8 February 1998 Eric Raymond publicly called on the community to replace the term "free software" with "open source", saying the group had come up with the label together.

I brainstormed this with some Silicon Valley fans of Linux (including Larry Augustin of the Linux International board of directors) the day after my meeting with Netscape (Feb 5th). We kicked around and discarded several alternatives, and we came up with a replacement label we all liked: "open source".

Primary source: own-post Goodbye, "free software"; hello, "open source", Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), by Eric S. Raymond, published 8 February 1998. Read 27 November 2026.

Raymond writes "we came up with"; he does not name Peterson in this passage. The date of 8 February is from his own preface to the document.

Connects: Eric S. Raymond

11 August 1998 The label

An internal Microsoft memorandum of August 1998 judged open source software to be a direct, short-term revenue and platform threat to the company.

OSS poses a direct, short-term revenue and platform threat to Microsoft, particularly in server space.

Primary source: document Halloween Document I (Microsoft memorandum "Open Source Software: A (New?) Development Methodology", annotated), Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), by Vinod Valloppillil (memorandum); Eric S. Raymond (annotations), published 1 November 1998. Read 27 November 2026.

The memorandum (author Vinod Valloppillil, dated "Aug 11, 1998 -- v1.00" in the text) was leaked to Eric Raymond, who published it with his annotations on 1 November 1998. It is read here in Raymond's annotated copy; Microsoft has not published the original.

Connects: Vinod Valloppillil, Eric S. Raymond, Microsoft, Halloween Document I, Leak of the Halloween Documents

Link to this fact

November 1998 The label

According to Raymond, Microsoft admitted that the leaked memorandum was authentic within days of its publication.

Pressed, Microsoft admitted the authenticity of Halloween I later on Monday

Primary source: own-post Halloween Document III: Microsoft's Reaction to the "Halloween Memorandum", Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), by Eric S. Raymond, published 5 November 1998. Read 27 November 2026.

Usually paraphrased. This is Raymond's statement, written on 5 November 1998, not Microsoft's own words. The same page reproduces a response attributed to Microsoft Netherlands. No Microsoft press statement from 1998 could be fetched.

Connects: Eric S. Raymond, Microsoft, Leak of the Halloween Documents

17 February 1999 The label

In the same message Perens said he would go on promoting software that fits the Open Source Definition, but independently of the Open Source Initiative.

I'll be working to promote software that fits that definition, but independently from the Open Source Initiative.

Primary source: mailing-list It's Time to Talk about Free Software Again (email to the editor, as published), LWN.net, by Bruce Perens, published 17 February 1999. Read 27 November 2026.

Connects: Bruce Perens, Eric S. Raymond, Open Source Initiative, It's Time to Talk about Free Software Again

July 2000 The label

Fowler picked out the same point Raymond had made in "Linus's Law": in open source, debugging can be done by many people in parallel.

A particular feature of open source development is that debugging is highly parallelizable.

Primary source: own-post The New Methodology (Original), martinfowler.com, by Martin Fowler, published 21 July 2000. Read 27 November 2026.

Connects: Martin Fowler, Eric S. Raymond, The New Methodology, The Cathedral and the Bazaar
