# From shared software to free software to open source

How a habit became a movement and then a label, and why the people who started the movement did not all accept the label.

### 1955: In the early mainframe years IBM supplied its software as source code, and SHARE members modified it and exchanged their changes through the SHARE library.

Step 1. Software begins as something users exchange in source form.

> Originally, IBM distributed what software it provided in source form [...] and systems programmers commonly made small local additions or modifications and exchanged them with other users.

- Source (secondary, encyclopaedia): [SHARE (computing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SHARE_(computing)), Wikipedia. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Encyclopaedia entry used as a last resort: no primary SHARE or IBM document on the library could be fetched. The date is the founding year of SHARE, not of a particular exchange.
- Connects: SHARE, IBM
- Fact id: `share-library-source-form`

### 1971: Richard Stallman recalls joining the MIT Artificial Intelligence Lab in 1971 and finding a community in which sharing software was the normal practice.

Step 2. Stallman finds the same habit at the MIT AI Lab and later takes it as his model.

> I became part of a software-sharing community that had existed for many years. Sharing of software was not limited to our particular community; it is as old as computers, just as sharing of recipes is as old as cooking.

- Source (primary, own-post): [The GNU Project](http://www.gnu.org/gnu/thegnuproject.html), GNU Project (Free Software Foundation), published 1998. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: This is Stallman's own later recollection (essay first published in 1998), not a document from 1971.
- Connects: Richard Stallman, MIT Artificial Intelligence Lab
- Fact id: `stallman-sharing-community-1971`

### 3 February 1976: In February 1976 Bill Gates wrote an open letter accusing computer hobbyists of stealing software, setting out the case that software should be paid for rather than shared.

Step 3. Software becomes a product, and sharing it is called theft.

> As the majority of hobbyists must be aware, most of you steal your software. Hardware must be paid for, but software is something to share. Who cares if the people who worked on it get paid?

- Source (primary, document): [An Open Letter to Hobbyists](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Open_Letter_to_Hobbyists), Wikisource (transcription of the letter), published 3 February 1976. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Quoted from the Wikisource transcription of the letter, which is dated February 3, 1976 in the text.
- Connects: Bill Gates, An Open Letter to Hobbyists
- Fact id: `gates-open-letter-1976`

### 27 September 1983: On 27 September 1983 Richard Stallman announced on Usenet that he would write a complete Unix-compatible system called GNU and give it away.

Step 4. Stallman answers by announcing a complete free system, GNU.

> Starting this Thanksgiving I am going to write a complete Unix-compatible software system called GNU (for Gnu's Not Unix)

- Source (primary, mailing-list): [Initial Announcement (new Unix implementation)](http://www.gnu.org/gnu/initial-announcement.html), GNU Project (Free Software Foundation), published 27 September 1983. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The GNU Project page that hosts the text states that it was "posted by Richard Stallman on September 27, 1983". gnu.org was reachable only over http from this machine.
- Connects: Richard Stallman, GNU Project, GNU Initial Announcement, Announcement of the GNU Project
- Fact id: `gnu-announced-1983`

### 27 September 1983: Stallman gave an ethical reason for GNU: he held that sharing programs is a duty and refused to sign nondisclosure or software licence agreements.

Step 5. His reason is ethical, not technical.

> I consider that the golden rule requires that if I like a program I must share it with other people who like it. I cannot in good conscience sign a nondisclosure agreement or a software license agreement.

- Source (primary, mailing-list): [Initial Announcement (new Unix implementation)](http://www.gnu.org/gnu/initial-announcement.html), GNU Project (Free Software Foundation), published 27 September 1983. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Richard Stallman, GNU Initial Announcement
- Fact id: `gnu-golden-rule-1983`

### March 1985: The GNU Manifesto of 1985 set out the rule that later became copyleft: anyone may modify and redistribute GNU, but nobody may restrict its further redistribution.

Step 6. The Manifesto states the rule that redistribution may never be restricted.

> Everyone will be permitted to modify and redistribute GNU, but no distributor will be allowed to restrict its further redistribution.

- Source (primary, document): [The GNU Manifesto](http://www.gnu.org/gnu/manifesto.html), GNU Project (Free Software Foundation), published 1985. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The month, March 1985, is given by the GNU Project's overview page ("the GNU Manifesto was published in March 1985"); the manifesto page itself says only 1985.
- Connects: Richard Stallman, GNU Project, The GNU Manifesto
- Fact id: `gnu-manifesto-1985`

### October 1985: The Free Software Foundation was founded in October 1985, at first to raise money for the development of GNU.

Step 7. The Free Software Foundation gives the project an organisation.

> The Free Software Foundation was founded in October 1985, initially to raise funds to help develop GNU.

- Source (primary, document): [Overview of the GNU System](http://www.gnu.org/gnu/gnu-history.html), GNU Project (Free Software Foundation). Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Free Software Foundation, GNU Project
- Fact id: `fsf-founded-1985`

### February 1989: Version 1 of the GNU General Public License, dated February 1989, states its purpose as guaranteeing the freedom to share and change free software.

Step 8. The GPL turns the rule into a licence.

> our General Public License is intended to guarantee your freedom to share and change free software--to make sure the software is free for all its users.

- Source (primary, licence): [GNU General Public License, version 1](http://www.gnu.org/licenses/old-licenses/gpl-1.0.txt), Free Software Foundation, published February 1989. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Free Software Foundation, GNU General Public License
- Fact id: `gpl-v1-1989`

### 25 August 1991: On 25 August 1991 Linus Torvalds told the comp.os.minix newsgroup that he was writing a free operating system as a hobby.

Step 9. A student announces a kernel as a hobby.

> I'm doing a (free) operating system (just a hobby, won't be big and professional like gnu) for 386(486) AT clones.

- Source (primary, archive): [Linux History (compilation of early Usenet posts on comp.os.minix)](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~awb/linux.history.html), Carnegie Mellon University School of Computer Science (personal page), published 25 August 1991. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Read in a compilation of the Usenet posts hosted at Carnegie Mellon University, which shows the header "Date: 25 Aug 91 20:57:08 GMT". The original is a Usenet post; Google Groups could not be fetched as text.
- Connects: Linus Torvalds
- Fact id: `linus-minix-post-1991`

### 1992: In the release notes for Linux 0.12 Torvalds announced that the kernel would move to the GNU copyleft, dropping his earlier ban on distributing it for money.

Step 10. He puts it under the GNU copyleft.

> The Linux copyright will change: I've had a couple of requests to make it compatible with the GNU copyleft, removing the "you may not distribute it for money" condition. I agree.

- Source (primary, document): [Release notes for Linux v0.12](https://www.kernel.org/pub/linux/kernel/Historic/old-versions/RELNOTES-0.12), kernel.org (Historic old versions). Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The release notes say the GNU copyleft "takes effect as of the first of February" but carry no year; 1992 is from the GNU Project overview, which says Linux was "made free software in 1992".
- Connects: Linus Torvalds, GNU General Public License
- Fact id: `linux-gpl-1992`

### 1992: The GNU Project says that by 1990 it had every major component of its system except the kernel, and that Linux filled that gap from 1992.

Step 11. GNU and Linux together make a complete free system.

> By 1990 we had either found or written all the major components except one—the kernel. Then Linux, a Unix-like kernel, was developed by Linus Torvalds in 1991 and made free software in 1992.

- Source (primary, document): [Overview of the GNU System](http://www.gnu.org/gnu/gnu-history.html), GNU Project (Free Software Foundation). Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Linus Torvalds, GNU Project
- Fact id: `gnu-plus-linux-1992`

### 1996: The Free Software Definition says a program is free software when its users have four essential freedoms: to run it, to study and change it, to redistribute copies and to distribute modified versions.

Step 12. Free software is defined by four freedoms.

> A program is free software if the program's users (referred to individually here as “you”) have the four essential freedoms

- Source (primary, document): [What is Free Software? (The Free Software Definition)](http://www.gnu.org/philosophy/free-sw.html), GNU Project (Free Software Foundation). Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The definition is a living document; the quote is from the current text. Its footnote says "Around 1990 there were three freedoms, numbered 1, 2 and 3", with freedom 0 added later, so the wording has changed over time. The date is the first copyright year on the page (1996), not the date of the idea.
- Connects: Free Software Foundation, GNU Project, The Free Software Definition
- Fact id: `four-freedoms`

### 5 July 1997: The Debian Social Contract and its Debian Free Software Guidelines were drafted by Bruce Perens, refined by Debian developers in June 1997 and ratified as version 1.0 on 5 July 1997.

Step 13. Debian writes down what counts as free.

> This document was drafted by Bruce Perens, refined by the other Debian developers during a month-long e-mail conference in June 1997, and then accepted as the publicly stated policy of the Debian Project.

- Source (primary, document): [Debian Social Contract (with the Debian Free Software Guidelines)](https://www.debian.org/social_contract), Debian Project, published 5 July 1997. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The ratification date is from the head of the same page: "Version 1.0 ratified on July 5, 1997."
- Connects: Bruce Perens, Debian Project, Debian Social Contract, Debian Free Software Guidelines
- Fact id: `debian-social-contract-1997`

### May 1997: Eric Raymond's essay "The Cathedral and the Bazaar", first presented in May 1997, contrasted closed "cathedral" development with the open "bazaar" style of Linux.

Step 14. Raymond explains the method to outsiders.

> I discuss these theories in terms of two fundamentally different development styles, the ``cathedral'' model of most of the commercial world versus the ``bazaar'' model of the Linux world.

- Source (primary, document): [The Cathedral and the Bazaar (title page, revision history and abstract)](http://catb.org/~esr/writings/cathedral-bazaar/cathedral-bazaar/index.html), Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), published May 1997. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The date is from the essay's revision history, which records the first official presentation at the Linux Kongress in May 1997. The text read is version 3.0 (2000). www.catb.org did not answer; catb.org over http did.
- Connects: Eric S. Raymond, Linus Torvalds, The Cathedral and the Bazaar
- Fact id: `cathedral-bazaar-1997`

### February 1998: Christine Peterson, then executive director of the Foresight Institute, says she came up with the term "open source software" in the first week of February 1998.

Step 15. A new name is proposed for the same software.

> Between meetings that week, I was still focused on the need for a better name and came up with the term "open source software."

- Source (primary, own-post): [How I coined the term 'open source'](https://opensource.com/article/18/2/coining-term-open-source-software), Opensource.com (Red Hat), published 1 February 2018. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Her account was written on 2 January 2006 and published on Opensource.com in February 2018.
- Connects: Christine Peterson, Foresight Institute
- Fact id: `peterson-coins-open-source-1998`

### 8 February 1998: Raymond's stated reason for the new label was pragmatic: "free software" was ambiguous and made business people nervous.

Step 16. The case for the new name is that "free" confuses and alarms business.

> Second, the term makes a lot of corporate types nervous. While this does not intrinsically bother me in the least, we now have a pragmatic interest in converting these people rather than thumbing our noses at them.

- Source (primary, own-post): [Goodbye, "free software"; hello, "open source"](http://catb.org/~esr/open-source.html), Eric S. Raymond (catb.org), published 8 February 1998. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Eric S. Raymond
- Fact id: `raymond-free-software-term-has-to-go`

### February 1998: The Open Source Definition was made in February 1998 by revising the Debian Free Software Guidelines and removing the references to Debian.

Step 17. The Open Source Definition is Debian's guidelines with Debian removed.

> The Open Source Definition was then created during the launch of the OSI in Feb. 1998 by revising the DFSG and removing Debian-specific references.

- Source (primary, document): [History of the OSI](https://opensource.org/history), Open Source Initiative. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Bruce Perens, Open Source Initiative, Debian Project, The Open Source Definition, Debian Free Software Guidelines
- Fact id: `osd-derived-from-dfsg-1998`

### 1998: Richard Stallman describes 1998 as the year part of the free software community splintered off to campaign under the name "open source".

Step 18. Stallman calls this a splintering of the community.

> In 1998, a part of the free software community splintered off and began campaigning in the name of “open source.”

- Source (primary, own-post): [Why Open Source Misses the Point of Free Software](http://www.gnu.org/philosophy/open-source-misses-the-point.html), GNU Project (Free Software Foundation), published 2007. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: From Stallman's essay, whose copyright notice begins in 2007.
- Connects: Richard Stallman, Why Open Source Misses the Point of Free Software
- Fact id: `stallman-1998-splinter`

### 17 February 1999: A year after co-founding the OSI, Bruce Perens wrote that open source had played down the freedoms of free software and that it was time to talk about free software again.

Step 19. A co-founder of the OSI says the freedoms have been played down.

> Most hackers know that Free Software and Open Source are just two words for the same thing. Unfortunately, though, Open Source has de-emphasized the importance of the freedoms involved in Free Software.

- Source (primary, mailing-list): [It's Time to Talk about Free Software Again (email to the editor, as published)](https://static.lwn.net/1999/0218/a/bp.html), LWN.net, published 17 February 1999. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Read in the copy of his email of 17 February 1999 published by LWN.net; the Debian mailing-list archive presented a proof-of-work challenge and could not be read.
- Connects: Bruce Perens, Open Source Initiative, It's Time to Talk about Free Software Again
- Fact id: `perens-free-software-again-1999`

### 2007: Stallman's position is that free software is a movement for users' freedom while open source values mainly practical advantage, and that the GNU Project therefore does not use the term "open source".

Step 20. The free software position, in Stallman's words.

> The free software movement campaigns for freedom for the users of computing; it is a movement for freedom and justice. By contrast, the open source idea values mainly practical advantage and does not campaign for principles. This is why we do not agree with open source, and do not use that term.

- Source (primary, own-post): [Why Open Source Misses the Point of Free Software](http://www.gnu.org/philosophy/open-source-misses-the-point.html), GNU Project (Free Software Foundation), published 2007. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Dated to the first copyright year of the essay (2007); the essay has been revised since and the current text was read.
- Connects: Richard Stallman, Free Software Foundation, Why Open Source Misses the Point of Free Software
- Fact id: `stallman-open-source-misses-point`

### 29 June 2007: The Free Software Foundation released version 3 of the GPL on 29 June 2007, adding protection against locked-down devices and an explicit patent licence.

Step 21. The GPL is revised to defend those freedoms against patents and locked devices.

> Version 3 of the GNU GPL strengthens this guarantee, by ensuring that users can modify the free software on their personal and household devices, and granting patent licenses to every user.

- Source (primary, press-release): [FSF releases the GNU General Public License, version 3](https://www.fsf.org/news/gplv3_launched), Free Software Foundation, published 29 June 2007. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Richard Stallman, Free Software Foundation, GNU General Public License
- Fact id: `gplv3-released-2007`
