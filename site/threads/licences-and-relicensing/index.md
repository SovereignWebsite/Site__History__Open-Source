# Licences and relicensing

The licences are the mechanism. This thread follows the two families, copyleft and permissive, the definitions built on them, and the recent moves away from both.

### March 1985: The GNU Manifesto of 1985 set out the rule that later became copyleft: anyone may modify and redistribute GNU, but nobody may restrict its further redistribution.

Step 1. The rule behind copyleft is stated.

> Everyone will be permitted to modify and redistribute GNU, but no distributor will be allowed to restrict its further redistribution.

- Source (primary, document): [The GNU Manifesto](http://www.gnu.org/gnu/manifesto.html), GNU Project (Free Software Foundation), published 1985. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The month, March 1985, is given by the GNU Project's overview page ("the GNU Manifesto was published in March 1985"); the manifesto page itself says only 1985.
- Connects: Richard Stallman, GNU Project, The GNU Manifesto
- Fact id: `gnu-manifesto-1985`

### 1989: Stallman describes copyleft, the method behind the GNU General Public License, as using copyright law to keep a program free.

Step 2. Copyleft: copyright used to keep a program free.

> Copyleft uses copyright law, but flips it over to serve the opposite of its usual purpose: instead of a means for restricting a program, it becomes a means for keeping the program free.

- Source (primary, own-post): [The GNU Project](http://www.gnu.org/gnu/thegnuproject.html), GNU Project (Free Software Foundation), published 1998. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The explanation is from Stallman's 1998 essay; it is dated here to the first GPL (1989), the licence that implements it.
- Connects: Richard Stallman, GNU General Public License
- Fact id: `copyleft-defined`

### February 1989: Version 1 of the GNU General Public License, dated February 1989, states its purpose as guaranteeing the freedom to share and change free software.

Step 3. GPL version 1.

> our General Public License is intended to guarantee your freedom to share and change free software--to make sure the software is free for all its users.

- Source (primary, licence): [GNU General Public License, version 1](http://www.gnu.org/licenses/old-licenses/gpl-1.0.txt), Free Software Foundation, published February 1989. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Free Software Foundation, GNU General Public License
- Fact id: `gpl-v1-1989`

### June 1989: In June 1989 Berkeley released Networking Release 1, its first freely redistributable code.

Step 4. Berkeley's first freely redistributable release.

> The BSD originated networking code and supporting utilities were released in June 1989 as Networking Release 1, the first freely-redistributable code from Berkeley.

- Source (primary, own-post): [Twenty Years of Berkeley Unix: From AT&T-Owned to Freely Redistributable (in Open Sources: Voices from the Open Source Revolution)](https://www.oreilly.com/openbook/opensources/book/kirkmck.html), O'Reilly, published January 1999. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: University of California, Berkeley (Computer Systems Research Group), BSD licence
- Fact id: `bsd-networking-release-1989`

### June 1991: Version 2 of the GNU General Public License is dated June 1991.

Step 5. GPL version 2, the licence Linux adopts.

> Version 2, June 1991

- Source (primary, licence): [GNU General Public License, version 2](http://www.gnu.org/licenses/old-licenses/gpl-2.0.txt), Free Software Foundation, published June 1991. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Free Software Foundation, GNU General Public License
- Fact id: `gpl-v2-1991`

### 1992: In the release notes for Linux 0.12 Torvalds announced that the kernel would move to the GNU copyleft, dropping his earlier ban on distributing it for money.

Step 6. Linux moves to the GNU copyleft.

> The Linux copyright will change: I've had a couple of requests to make it compatible with the GNU copyleft, removing the "you may not distribute it for money" condition. I agree.

- Source (primary, document): [Release notes for Linux v0.12](https://www.kernel.org/pub/linux/kernel/Historic/old-versions/RELNOTES-0.12), kernel.org (Historic old versions). Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The release notes say the GNU copyleft "takes effect as of the first of February" but carry no year; 1992 is from the GNU Project overview, which says Linux was "made free software in 1992".
- Connects: Linus Torvalds, GNU General Public License
- Fact id: `linux-gpl-1992`

### June 1994: 4.4BSD-Lite, released in June 1994, carried the permissive Berkeley terms: free redistribution in source and binary form, provided the copyright notices and credit to the University remain.

Step 7. The permissive alternative: keep the notices and the credit.

> the terms allow free redistribution in source and binary form subject only to the constraint that the University copyrights remain intact and that the University receive credit when others use the code.

- Source (primary, own-post): [Twenty Years of Berkeley Unix: From AT&T-Owned to Freely Redistributable (in Open Sources: Voices from the Open Source Revolution)](https://www.oreilly.com/openbook/opensources/book/kirkmck.html), O'Reilly, published January 1999. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: University of California, Berkeley (Computer Systems Research Group), BSD licence
- Fact id: `bsd-licence-terms-1994`

### 5 July 1997: The Debian Social Contract and its Debian Free Software Guidelines were drafted by Bruce Perens, refined by Debian developers in June 1997 and ratified as version 1.0 on 5 July 1997.

Step 8. Debian's guidelines test whether a licence is free.

> This document was drafted by Bruce Perens, refined by the other Debian developers during a month-long e-mail conference in June 1997, and then accepted as the publicly stated policy of the Debian Project.

- Source (primary, document): [Debian Social Contract (with the Debian Free Software Guidelines)](https://www.debian.org/social_contract), Debian Project, published 5 July 1997. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The ratification date is from the head of the same page: "Version 1.0 ratified on July 5, 1997."
- Connects: Bruce Perens, Debian Project, Debian Social Contract, Debian Free Software Guidelines
- Fact id: `debian-social-contract-1997`

### 22 January 1998: Netscape said its source licence would allow modification and redistribution and would build on the heritage of the GPL.

Step 9. Netscape writes a new licence in the GPL's tradition.

> The company will handle free source distribution with a license which allows source code modification and redistribution and provides for free availability of source code versions, building on the heritage of the GNU Public License (GPL), familiar to developers on the Net.

- Source (primary, press-release): [January 22, 1998: the Beginning of Mozilla (reproduces the Netscape press release in full)](https://blog.lizardwrangler.com/?p=187), Mitchell Baker's blog (Lizard Wrangling), published 22 January 1998. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Same reproduction of the press release as above. The release says "GNU Public License"; the licence's name is the GNU General Public License.
- Connects: Netscape Communications, GNU General Public License, Netscape source code announcement
- Fact id: `netscape-licence-gpl-heritage-1998`

### February 1998: The Open Source Definition was made in February 1998 by revising the Debian Free Software Guidelines and removing the references to Debian.

Step 10. The Open Source Definition becomes the test for "open source".

> The Open Source Definition was then created during the launch of the OSI in Feb. 1998 by revising the DFSG and removing Debian-specific references.

- Source (primary, document): [History of the OSI](https://opensource.org/history), Open Source Initiative. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Bruce Perens, Open Source Initiative, Debian Project, The Open Source Definition, Debian Free Software Guidelines
- Fact id: `osd-derived-from-dfsg-1998`

### 1998: Criterion 6 of the Open Source Definition forbids a licence from restricting the fields in which a program may be used, including use in a business.

Step 11. Its sixth criterion: no restriction on fields of use.

> The license must not restrict anyone from making use of the program in a specific field of endeavor. For example, it may not restrict the program from being used in a business, or from being used for genetic research.

- Source (primary, document): [The Open Source Definition](https://opensource.org/osd), Open Source Initiative. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: Quoted from the current text of the definition. This is the clause the OSI cites against licences that restrict cloud providers.
- Connects: Open Source Initiative, The Open Source Definition
- Fact id: `osd-no-field-discrimination`

### 3 May 2001: On 3 May 2001 Craig Mundie of Microsoft told an audience at New York University's Stern School of Business that the "viral" GPL threatened the intellectual property of any organisation using it.

Step 12. The GPL is attacked as viral.

> This viral aspect of the GPL poses a threat to the intellectual property of any organization making use of it.

- Source (primary, speech): [Speech Transcript: Craig Mundie, The New York University Stern School of Business (Prepared Text of Remarks: The Commercial Software Model)](https://news.microsoft.com/speeches/speech-transcript-craig-mundie-the-new-york-university-stern-school-of-business/), Microsoft, published 3 May 2001. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: From the prepared text of the remarks as published by Microsoft; it is not a verbatim record of the delivery.
- Connects: Craig Mundie, Microsoft, GNU General Public License, Craig Mundie's speech at NYU Stern
- Fact id: `mundie-gpl-viral-2001`

### November 2006: In November 2006 Sun Microsystems committed to releasing Java under the GPL, a move the Free Software Foundation welcomed.

Step 13. A large company chooses the GPL for Java.

> The Free Software Foundation (FSF) welcomed the public commitment from Sun Microsystems to distribute its proprietary Java platform under the GNU General Public License (GPL)

- Source (primary, press-release): [Sun begins releasing Java under the GPL](https://www.fsf.org/news/fsf-welcomes-gpl-java.html), Free Software Foundation, published 15 November 2006. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: This is the FSF's press release of 15 November 2006, primary for the FSF's reaction and second-hand for Sun's announcement; Sun's own release (13 November 2006 by other accounts) is no longer online and could not be fetched.
- Connects: Richard Stallman, Sun Microsystems, Free Software Foundation, GNU General Public License
- Fact id: `sun-java-gpl-2006`

### 29 June 2007: The Free Software Foundation released version 3 of the GPL on 29 June 2007, adding protection against locked-down devices and an explicit patent licence.

Step 14. GPL version 3 adds patent and device provisions.

> Version 3 of the GNU GPL strengthens this guarantee, by ensuring that users can modify the free software on their personal and household devices, and granting patent licenses to every user.

- Source (primary, press-release): [FSF releases the GNU General Public License, version 3](https://www.fsf.org/news/gplv3_launched), Free Software Foundation, published 29 June 2007. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Richard Stallman, Free Software Foundation, GNU General Public License
- Fact id: `gplv3-released-2007`

### 16 October 2018: On 16 October 2018 MongoDB moved its Community Server from the AGPL to a new licence of its own, the Server Side Public License.

Step 15. MongoDB writes the SSPL to reach cloud providers.

> issued a new software license, called Server Side Public License (SSPL), for MongoDB Community Server.

- Source (primary, press-release): [MongoDB Issues New Server Side Public License for MongoDB Community Server](https://www.mongodb.com/company/newsroom/press-releases/mongodb-issues-new-server-side-public-license-for-mongodb-community-server), MongoDB, Inc., published 16 October 2018. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The same release says MongoDB "submitted it to the Open Source Initiative for approval".
- Connects: MongoDB, Inc., Open Source Initiative, Server Side Public License
- Fact id: `mongodb-sspl-2018`

### 2018: MongoDB's own FAQ states that the SSPL has not been approved by the Open Source Initiative.

Step 16. The SSPL is not OSI-approved.

> The SSPL has not been approved by the OSI.

- Source (primary, document): [Server Side Public License FAQ](https://www.mongodb.com/legal/licensing/server-side-public-license/faq), MongoDB, Inc.. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The FAQ is undated; it is dated here to the year the licence was issued.
- Connects: MongoDB, Inc., Open Source Initiative, Server Side Public License
- Fact id: `sspl-not-osi-approved`

### 14 January 2021: On 14 January 2021 Elastic moved Elasticsearch and Kibana from the Apache 2.0 licence to a choice of the SSPL or its own Elastic License.

Step 17. Elastic leaves the Apache licence.

> We are moving our Apache 2.0-licensed source code in Elasticsearch and Kibana to be dual licensed under Server Side Public License (SSPL) and the Elastic License, giving users the choice of which license to apply.

- Source (primary, own-post): [Doubling down on open, Part II](https://www.elastic.co/blog/licensing-change), Elastic, published 14 January 2021. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Shay Banon, Elastic, Server Side Public License
- Fact id: `elastic-licence-change-2021`

### 19 January 2021: On 19 January 2021 the Open Source Initiative stated that the SSPL is not an open source licence, calling such licences "fauxpen" source.

Step 18. The OSI says the SSPL is not open source.

> Fauxpen source licenses allow a user to view the source code but do not allow other highly important rights protected by the Open Source Definition, such as the right to make use of the program for any field of endeavor.

- Source (primary, press-release): [The SSPL is Not an Open Source License](https://opensource.org/blog/the-sspl-is-not-an-open-source-license), Open Source Initiative, published 19 January 2021. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Open Source Initiative, Elastic, MongoDB, Inc., Server Side Public License, The Open Source Definition
- Fact id: `osi-sspl-not-open-source-2021`

### 19 January 2021: The OSI says the SSPL was submitted for its approval and then withdrawn when it became clear it would not be approved.

Step 19. The SSPL had been withdrawn from OSI review.

> This license was submitted to the Open Source Initiative for approval but later withdrawn by the license steward when it became clear that the license would not be approved.

- Source (primary, press-release): [The SSPL is Not an Open Source License](https://opensource.org/blog/the-sspl-is-not-an-open-source-license), Open Source Initiative, published 19 January 2021. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Open Source Initiative, MongoDB, Inc., Server Side Public License
- Fact id: `osi-sspl-withdrawn`

### 10 August 2023: On 10 August 2023 HashiCorp changed the licence of Terraform from the Mozilla Public License to the Business Source License.

Step 20. HashiCorp moves Terraform to the Business Source License.

> HashiCorp switched the license for Terraform from the MPL to the Business Source License (v1.1) (the “BUSL”), a non-open source license.

- Source (primary, document): [The OpenTofu Manifesto](https://opentofu.org/manifesto/), OpenTofu, published 2023. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: This is the wording of the OpenTofu manifesto, written by those who objected to the change; "a non-open source license" is their description. HashiCorp's own announcement returned 429 on every attempt and could not be read. The licence file in the Terraform repository, which was read, is headed Business Source License and now names IBM as licensor.
- Connects: HashiCorp, OpenTofu, Business Source License 1.1
- Fact id: `hashicorp-busl-2023`

### 20 September 2023: On 20 September 2023 the Linux Foundation announced OpenTofu as an open source alternative to Terraform.

Step 21. OpenTofu is launched as an open source alternative, from a fork of Terraform.

> Today, the Linux Foundation announced the formation of OpenTofu, an open source alternative to Terraform's widely used infrastructure as code provisioning tool.

- Source (primary, press-release): [Linux Foundation Launches OpenTofu: A New Open Source Alternative to Terraform](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/press/announcing-opentofu), The Linux Foundation, published 20 September 2023. Read 27 November 2026.
- Note: The OpenTofu manifesto, also read, says: "we created a fork of Terraform".
- Connects: The Linux Foundation, OpenTofu, HashiCorp, Business Source License 1.1
- Fact id: `opentofu-2023`

### 20 March 2024: On 20 March 2024 Redis announced that future versions would no longer be under the BSD licence but under two source-available licences.

Step 22. Redis leaves the BSD licence.

> Starting with Redis 7.4, Redis will be dual-licensed under the Redis Source Available License (RSALv2) and Server Side Public License (SSPLv1). Consequently, Redis will no longer be distributed under the three-clause Berkeley Software Distribution (BSD).

- Source (primary, own-post): [Redis Adopts Dual Source-Available Licensing](https://redis.io/blog/redis-adopts-dual-source-available-licensing/), Redis, published 20 March 2024. Read 27 November 2026.
- Connects: Redis, Server Side Public License, BSD licence
- Fact id: `redis-licence-change-2024`

Step 26. Redis concedes the SSPL is not truly open source.

> SSPL is not truly open source because the Open Source Initiative clarified it lacks the requisites to be an OSI-approved license.
